Three approaches, three seeds, one small neural network. Compare full-trajectory imitation, backward curriculum, and corrective demonstrations validated by teacher recovery tests.

Initial comparisons use 24,000 supervised updates; continuations from the best six-second controller use another 48,000. All methods rehearse balancing throughout. No PPO in this phase. The candidate keeps training through regressions; a separate best checkpoint remains available. At runtime the actor sees only joint angles and velocities—no clock, teacher, or LQR.

Success means balancing for the final ten seconds of a 45-second episode. Curriculum progression requires two consecutive development checks with ≥90% balance and ≥80% approach success. Pilot audits use 64 starts per condition and seed. Continuations reuse that benchmark; their separate fresh confirmations use 128 new starts. Approach success does not imply bottom-start success. The current training start is not a claim of mastery. A selected “initial” checkpoint means training did not produce a better trusted policy.

What the imitation runs showed

Full-trajectory imitation failed. Backward training plus validated corrections extended the learned approach from two seconds to eight seconds. In fresh 128-start confirmation tests, two trimmed policies passed 117/128 and 118/128 eight-second starts and retained 128/128 balancing.

Those same policies scored 0/128 on six-second starts: they specialized to particular maneuvers rather than mastering every later approach state. Every tested policy still failed bottom-start swing-up. Preserving balancing alone is not enough; earlier successful approaches need their own retention coverage.

These findings come from 24 imitation jobs. Three PPO follow-ups also completed: one early checkpoint passed 64/64 balancing, 64/64 six-second starts and 59/64 eight-second starts, but 0/64 bottom starts. Continued PPO then lost much of the approach skill. The full PPO results are linked below. All deployed controllers are neural-only.
